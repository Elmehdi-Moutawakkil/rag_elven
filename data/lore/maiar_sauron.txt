The Maiar: Servants of the Valar

The Maiar are angelic beings of the same nature as the Valar, but lesser in power. Like the Valar, they are Ainur — holy spirits created by Iluvatar before the world. They descended into Arda to assist the Valar in shaping and governing the world.

The name "Maiar" (singular: Maia) means roughly "the Beautiful Ones" or "the Adorned" in Quenya. While the Valar numbered fourteen, the Maiar are countless, though few are named in the recorded lore.

Each Maia typically served a specific Vala and shared in their master's domain. For example, Osse and Uinen served Ulmo, lord of waters; Melian served Varda and Yavanna before coming to Middle-earth; Eonwe served Manwe as his herald and banner-bearer.

The Maiar could take physical forms when they entered the world, though their true nature was spiritual. Those who took up a body in Arda became increasingly bound to the physical world over time.

---

Notable Maiar

Eonwe — Herald of Manwe and banner-bearer of the King of Arda. Considered among the mightiest of Maiar. He led the host of the Valar in the War of Wrath at the end of the First Age.

Melian — A Maia of great beauty and wisdom who served Varda and Yavanna. She came to Middle-earth long before the Elves awoke, filling the forests with her enchanting music. She fell in love with Thingol (Elwe), a lord of the Teleri Elves, and they together founded the kingdom of Doriath in Beleriand. She is the only Maia known to have wed one of the Children of Iluvatar. Their daughter was Luthien, the most beautiful of all Elves. Melian cast the Girdle of Melian around Doriath, a magical barrier that prevented enemies from entering without her will.

Osse — A Maia who served Ulmo, lord of waters. He loved the seas of Middle-earth so deeply that he did not journey to Valinor with the other Maiar. He delights in storms and the turbulent sea. He was once swayed by Melkor's influence but was recalled by Uinen.

Uinen — The Lady of the Seas, consort of Osse. She calms the storms that Osse raises and has mercy on mariners. The Numenoreans revered her greatly.

Arien — The Maia chosen by the Valar to guide the Sun across the sky after the destruction of the Two Trees. She is a spirit of fire, too bright and fierce for Morgoth to corrupt, though he desired to capture her.

Tilion — The Maia who steers the Moon. He was a hunter in the service of Orome and carries a silver bow. He follows Arien because he loves her, though she does not return his feelings.

Sauron: The Lieutenant of Morgoth

Sauron (Quenya: Sauron, "the Abhorred"; earlier name Mairon, "the Admirable") was a Maia who served Aule, the craftsman of the Valar, before his corruption. He was among the most gifted of the Maiar, possessing great skill in craft, order, and understanding of complex systems.

In the beginning, Sauron was drawn to Morgoth's promises of power and order. Morgoth claimed he could eliminate chaos and achieve complete mastery of Arda. Sauron, who loved order and perfection, was persuaded. He became Morgoth's most powerful lieutenant and chief servant in the First Age.

During the First Age, Sauron served Morgoth faithfully as his general and spy-master. He held the fortress of Minas Tirith on the isle of Tol Sirion (later renamed Tol-in-Gaurhoth, the Isle of Werewolves) and commanded werewolves and vampires. He was defeated in single combat by the Hound of Valinor, Huan, when Beren and Luthien came to retrieve a Silmaril.

After Morgoth's defeat in the War of Wrath, Sauron initially repented before the herald Eonwe. But he refused to return to Valinor for judgment, fearing humiliation, and fled back into hiding in Middle-earth.

Sauron in the Second Age: The Lord of Gifts

After lying low for centuries, Sauron reappeared in Middle-earth during the Second Age, this time under a fair disguise. He called himself Annatar, "Lord of Gifts," and claimed to be an emissary of the Valar.

He came to the Elvish smiths of Eregion, led by Celebrimbor (grandson of Feanor), and taught them the craft of making Rings of Power. Under his instruction, the Elves forged the Seven Rings for Dwarf-lords and the Nine Rings for mortal men.

But secretly, in the fires of Mount Doom in Mordor, Sauron forged the One Ring — the Ruling Ring — which contained a great portion of his own power and will. Through it, he sought to dominate the minds of those who wore the lesser rings.

The Elves perceived his treachery when he put on the One Ring: they felt his mind reaching out to dominate them and immediately took off their rings. The Three Rings of the Elves (Narya, Nenya, Vilya) were hidden from Sauron and never came under his control.

The War of the Elves and Sauron (1693-1701 Second Age) nearly destroyed Eregion. Celebrimbor was slain. But the Rings of Power were hidden or scattered.

The One Ring and Sauron's Nature

The One Ring is not merely a weapon — it is a vessel for Sauron's own power and will. When he forged it, Sauron poured much of his native power into it. This is why, without the Ring, Sauron is greatly diminished; and why its destruction ultimately destroys him.

Sauron's physical form was first destroyed when Numenor was drowned (Second Age). He was unable to take a fair physical form again after that. In the Third Age, he appeared only as the Dark Lord of Mordor, a figure of terrible shadow and fire.

The Eye of Sauron — the great lidless eye of flame depicted in the Lord of the Rings — represents his searching gaze, not a literal eye. Sauron used the Palantiri (seeing stones) to project his will across Middle-earth.

Sauron's weakness is that his power is bound to the Ring. When Frodo destroyed it in the fires of Mount Doom (Third Age, year 3019), Sauron was unmade — not killed, for he was a Maia and his spirit could not be truly destroyed — but permanently reduced to a powerless shadow, unable to ever take form or influence the world again.

The Istari: The Five Wizards

The Istari (singular: Istar) were five Maiar sent to Middle-earth by the Valar at the beginning of the Third Age (approximately year 1000) to aid the free peoples in their resistance against Sauron. They were forbidden to use their full power or to dominate Men and Elves by force — their mission was to inspire, advise, and encourage, not to rule.

The Valar clothed them in the bodies of old men so they would not awe or terrify the peoples of Middle-earth. They were to remain humble and use only their wisdom and subtle influence.

The Five Istari:

Gandalf (Olorin in Valinor; Mithrandir in Sindarin, "Grey Pilgrim") — A Maia who served Manwe and Varda, and studied under Nienna, the Lady of Mercy. He came to Middle-earth reluctantly, the last of the five. He is the wisest and most humble of the Istari. He carried Narya, the Ring of Fire (one of the Three Elven Rings), given to him secretly by Cirdan the Shipwright. Gandalf alone among the Istari remained true to his mission. After his fall in Moria fighting the Balrog Durin's Bane, he was sent back as Gandalf the White, replacing Saruman as head of the order.

Saruman (Curunir in Sindarin, "Man of Skill"; Olorin: no equivalent named) — A Maia of Aule, the craftsman. He was the most powerful of the Istari and was appointed head of the order by the Valar. He was given the chief ring among the Istari, though not one of the great rings. Saruman's downfall came from his desire for the One Ring and his growing envy of Gandalf. He established himself in Isengard and bred the Uruk-hai. He was destroyed not by battle but by the curse of Saruman himself: thrown down by Grima Wormtongue, his spirit was refused entry to the West by the Valar and dissolved into the air.

Radagast (Aiwendil) — A Maia of Yavanna who loved animals and growing things above all. He dwelt at Rhosgobel on the edge of Mirkwood and communicated with birds and beasts. He was criticized by Saruman for caring more for nature than for the great struggle, but his friendship with the eagles and birds often proved crucial.

The Blue Wizards (Alatar and Pallando, or Morinehtar and Rómestámo) — The least known of the five. They went into the East of Middle-earth and little is recorded of their fate. Tolkien speculated in late writings that they succeeded in their mission by founding secret cults and movements in the East and South that weakened Sauron's dominion, though others fell into heresy or were forgotten.

The Istari and Free Will

A key element of the Istari's mission is that they were forbidden to dominate others. When Saruman used his voice to enslave the wills of others, or when he sought the One Ring for himself, he violated his mandate. The tragedy of Saruman is not just betrayal but the loss of wisdom: he became the very kind of dominating power he was sent to oppose.

Gandalf's strength was precisely in his restraint. He guided and advised but rarely commanded. Even when given the chance to take the One Ring himself, he refused — knowing that his good intentions would not prevent the Ring from corrupting him.
